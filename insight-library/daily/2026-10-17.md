# What landed today

Four cards, one new operator profile. Two from Addy Osmani, Morgan Westlee Lunt, and Qing Wang on October 15, from the Claude Code team's public write-up of their code review system. Two from Alex Cornell on October 12, from a Lenny Rachitsky interview about designing Meta AI's Muse product. Two lanes: agentic engineering and AI product design. Both sources ask the same underlying question: what do you simplify once AI gets fast and reliable enough?

## Agentic engineering: review moves earlier and filtering moves to consensus

The Claude Code team's October 15 post is a data-backed account of shipping review infrastructure at scale. The headline metric is the shift in where review happens: from after submission to during writing. `ins_osmani-review-runs-before-pr` captures the mechanism. Before a PR exists, Claude runs tests, builds, linters, and a subagent reviews the diff with fresh context. By the time a human looks at the PR, the change has already been through at least one review-and-fix cycle.

> "Now, review runs continuously, more like spellcheck while you write. Most changes have been reviewed and fixed by Claude at least once before a human is asked to look."

The result at Anthropic: substantive review comments rose from 16% to 54% after launch, mean reviews to pass fell from 2.08 to 1.32, and PRs needing five or more rounds dropped from 8.9% to 1.5%.

The second card, `ins_osmani-agent-consensus-finding-gate`, explains how they kept the quality of those comments high. Several agents review the same change independently. A separate group then votes on each finding. Only findings that pass the vote get posted. Style, diagnostics, and cleanup findings are dropped before the vote begins. The A/B test on their own PRs confirmed the filter was net-positive.

- Addy Osmani, Morgan Westlee Lunt, Qing Wang, `ins_osmani-review-runs-before-pr`. Moving review to writing time rather than post-submission raised substantive coverage from 16% to 54%.
- Addy Osmani, Morgan Westlee Lunt, Qing Wang, `ins_osmani-agent-consensus-finding-gate`. Multi-agent voting gates findings before they reach humans, filtering noise while raising signal.

## AI product design: what you can remove once models get fast

Alex Cornell's interview with Lenny Rachitsky on October 12 covers design decisions for Muse, Meta AI's personal agent. Two claims are worth isolating.

The first is about chain-of-thought display. `ins_cornell-cot-latency-workaround` Cornell's observation: showing reasoning steps is a workaround for slow models, not a product feature. Fast models skip it. The UX case for displaying reasoning was always about managing the wait, not about helping users understand the AI. When the wait disappears, so does the case.

The second is about how to structure AI-generated interfaces. `ins_cornell-fixed-structure-dynamic-content` Cornell separates what designers control (structural containers: avatar, card types, feed shapes) from what AI generates (text, suggestions, status emoji, small animations). The structure is fixed. The content varies. This keeps the design system intact and the user experience consistent across sessions, while still allowing personalization where it matters.

Both claims are about reducing complexity as model capability increases. Chain-of-thought display was a cost of slow models. Fully generative layouts are a cost of not trusting your design system. Cornell removed both.

- Alex Cornell, `ins_cornell-cot-latency-workaround`. Displaying chain-of-thought is a latency workaround; fast models should work quietly and deliver results.
- Alex Cornell, `ins_cornell-fixed-structure-dynamic-content`. Generative UI stays reliable when structure is fixed by designers and only content varies dynamically.
