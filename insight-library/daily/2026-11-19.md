# What landed today

Three cards, one new operator. All three come from Mihail Eric, adjunct professor at Stanford (CS146S: The Modern Software Developer) and head of AI at Monaco, in his Latent Space interview from early November 2026. The cards form a single through-line: as generation becomes cheaper, the remaining human advantages in engineering are comprehension, spec authorship, and the judgment to buy vs. build.

## Theme 1, Buying AI tools before building them is a learning discipline, not just a cost decision

Eric's rule, documented in `ins_eric-buy-to-learn-build-to-keep`, is clean: "buy to learn and you build to keep." Teams that jump to building before using commercial tools often build the wrong thing. The act of buying and using is how you discover what the gap actually is. At Monaco, Eric's team converged on Devin for code review through sustained use, then built their own agent workspaces only after finding no product on the market covered that specific need. The build decision followed domain knowledge, not the other way around.

The rule has an expiry condition: fast-moving markets mean a gap today may be a product next quarter. Eric's team builds when the use case is structurally tied to their own infrastructure or when the market has shown it won't serve that need. Otherwise, they buy and monitor.

- Mihail Eric, `ins_eric-buy-to-learn-build-to-keep`. Buy AI tools to learn the problem space, then build only when gaps are real and market offerings are unlikely to close them.

## Theme 2, Two human skills hold in a fully automated codebase: comprehension and spec-writing

Eric teaches CS146S at Stanford, where students contribute patches to live open-source projects with maintainer review. The course design reflects his core claim: comprehension is the prerequisite for delegation. As documented in `ins_eric-comprehension-pace-output`, agents introduce a different category of bugs from human programmers. Engineers who do not understand what was generated cannot catch those bugs in review. His formulation is precise: "once you have the language, you can prompt for it." Without the underlying understanding of a system, prompts become vague and reviews become superficial.

The companion claim, in `ins_eric-spec-choke-human-advantage`, extends this to the codebase level. Eric names spec-writing and code review as "the two choke points in a fully automated system." PR descriptions function as specs. Agents struggle to reconstruct whole-codebase intent from code alone. Humans remain what Eric calls "the spec gods" in complex systems, responsible for translating intent into the form agents can act on. The underlying observation connects to Thorsten Ball's Nov 8 work (`ins_ball-divergent-agent-timelines`) and Boris Cherny's earlier framing of review as the new throughput constraint (`ins_cherny-code-review-next-bottleneck`), but Eric names the spec step explicitly as an equal constraint alongside review.

> "those are the two choke points I think in, in a fully automated system."

- Mihail Eric, `ins_eric-comprehension-pace-output`. Agents introduce bugs humans would not make. Engineers must maintain language fluency and system comprehension to review what agents produce.
- Mihail Eric, `ins_eric-spec-choke-human-advantage`. Spec-writing and code review are the two irreplaceable human functions in a fully automated codebase.
