# What landed today

Four cards from four operators across three lanes: agentic engineering, product marketing, and game growth. Two sources are conference notes from the same event, one is a personal essay, and one is a data-backed blog post. The day's signal split roughly evenly between the AI practitioner lane and the non-AI lanes.

One quiet thread connects the AI cards: the value of architectural enforcement over advisory instruction. Yesterday's cards on dynamic workflows and budget enforcement made the same point at the session and harness level. Today's Huntley card makes it at the code-change level.

## Agentic engineering: enforcement beats instruction

Geoffrey Huntley published "nobody should type code by hand anymore. here's what's left." on October 13, 2026, at ghuntley.com. It is the clearest practitioner statement yet of a design pattern that has appeared in fragments across several earlier cards this week.

`ins_huntley-precommit-hook-agent-compliance` captures the mechanism. AGENTS.md and skill packs are probabilistic. They tell an agent what to do in natural language. Agents follow them sometimes, not others, and the files drift from the codebase as it evolves. A pre-commit hook is architectural. It runs deterministically on every commit the agent attempts, inspects the git diff for a specific violation, fails the commit when it finds one, and appends a corrective prompt. Huntley reports the pattern raising compliance from unreliable to ten out of ten for the specific architectural rule it guards.

The quote he uses to frame the larger shift:

> "software doesn't need to be readable anymore. it needs to be explainable."

This connects directly to yesterday's `ins_stall-budget-external-to-model`. Stall argued that budget limits must live outside the model's context window, not inside it, because disclosing the limit changes the model's reasoning. Huntley's hook works by the same principle: the enforcement mechanism is external to the agent's natural-language operating space.

- Geoffrey Huntley, `ins_huntley-precommit-hook-agent-compliance`. Pre-commit hooks enforce coding agent architectural compliance deterministically; AGENTS.md instructions cannot.

## PMM and product: what AI changes and what it does not

Mind the Product Chicago ran in early October 2026. Conference notes were published October 12. Two cards came out of the event.

April Dunford's talk is captured in `ins_dunford-ai-positioning-signals-noisier`. Her thesis: AI does not change what positioning is or how to do it. The fundamentals remain. What changes is the velocity at which the competitive landscape turns over, which makes the competitive signal noisier. Teams now regularly split on whether the real alternative is today's established tool or an emerging AI-era replacement. She names two failure modes from getting this wrong: talking about tomorrow's market before buyers recognize the problem (loss of credibility), and ignoring a shift until a competitor has already established the frame. Her practical prescription is to move from annual to quarterly positioning reviews and to segment messaging by customer AI maturity, since early adopters already live in tomorrow's competitive frame while mainstream buyers are still in today's.

`ins_simons-cheap-building-shifts-pm-value` captures Eric Simons, CEO of StackBlitz and Bolt, on what cheap building does to the product manager role. His argument is structural. Traditional PM value came from rationing scarce engineering hours. When building becomes cheap, prioritization loses most of its value because anyone can ship. The remaining defensible value is customer context: sustained proximity to real users, understanding why they behave as they do, running tight feedback loops. That knowledge cannot be generated by a model or copied by a team without the user relationships.

The two cards from this event sit in different lanes but share a common shape. Both argue that a prior rationing mechanism disappears with AI, and that what remains is judgment rooted in proximity: Dunford on proximity to the competitive landscape, Simons on proximity to customers.

- April Dunford, `ins_dunford-ai-positioning-signals-noisier`. AI accelerates competitive turnover and makes signals noisier; quarterly positioning reviews replace annual ones.
- Eric Simons, `ins_simons-cheap-building-shifts-pm-value`. Cheap building removes PM gatekeeping value; customer insight loop ownership is what remains.

## Game growth: inflation adjusts the benchmark, not the bet

Chris Zukowski published "Wishlist inflation is real, but they are still important" on October 13, 2026. The post is the most rigorous quantitative treatment of Steam wishlist dynamics the library has seen from him.

`ins_zukowski-wishlist-inflation-prediction-holds` captures the core finding. Wishlist inflation is real. Games released in 2023 to 2025 sold roughly half as many units per wishlist in their first month compared to 2020 to 2022 games. Valve made wishlisting easier over that period, adding hover-tooltip stars and festival opt-in mechanisms, and more developers ran active wishlist campaigns. Wishlists now function more like bookmarks.

But the relationship between wishlist count and launch sales held. Zukowski's regression across 160 games gives R² = 0.72 for recent games versus 0.77 for the earlier cohort, a small drop he attributes to noise. A game with twice the wishlists of a peer still launches to roughly twice the sales. The benchmark to hit for Popular Upcoming eligibility likely moved up, but wishlist count remains the best available leading indicator.

- Chris Zukowski, `ins_zukowski-wishlist-inflation-prediction-holds`. Steam wishlist inflation halved per-wishlist conversion since 2023 but did not break wishlist count as a launch sales predictor.
