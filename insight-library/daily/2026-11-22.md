# What landed today

Three cards, no new operators. Two themes emerged from independent sources this week: how public markets reprice AI companies once they cross the trillion-dollar threshold, and two independent examples of teams replacing multi-system infrastructure with a single unified layer.

## Theme 1, Valuation mechanics change structurally at $1T enterprise value

Tom Tunguz published a close analysis of Anthropic and SpaceX valuations on November 20. His core finding, documented in `ins_tunguz-10x-wall-earnings-cap`, is that public markets stop rewarding revenue growth once a company crosses roughly $1 trillion in enterprise value. The mechanism is arithmetic: a revenue multiple equals P/E multiple times net margin. At trillion-dollar scale, markets anchor to a 20x to 26x P/E band. With Microsoft at 41% net margins and 26x P/E, the implied cap on forward sales is approximately 10x. With Meta or Google margins around 25% and 20x P/E, it caps near 5x.

The practical result: Anthropic at $1.56T and SpaceX at $1.82T both trade at 9.5x 2027 estimated EV/revenue, despite implied growth rates of 288% and 466% respectively. Same multiple, different businesses, different growth rates. The growth rate stopped mattering at that scale.

> "Above $1t of enterprise value, public markets stop paying for top-line growth"

The implication for AI-native founders: reaching trillion-dollar scale does not extend the revenue-multiple runway. The question after that point shifts from "what multiple does our growth rate deserve" to "what are our earnings per share."

- Tom Tunguz, `ins_tunguz-10x-wall-earnings-cap`. Above $1T enterprise value, P/E multiples dominate valuation and cap revenue multiples regardless of growth rate.

## Theme 2, Replacing multi-system complexity with a single unified layer

Two posts from the same week, in completely different technical domains, described teams doing the same structural move: replacing a collection of specialized subsystems with one layer that handles all the cases.

Tom Tunguz's November 17 post on LanceDB, documented in `ins_tunguz-lakehouse-ai`, describes AI labs converging on a single columnar format for data that previously required separate blob storage, Parquet files, and vector indexes. The key efficiency is incremental column backfills: new data appends without rewriting prior rows, so only changed data moves. Exa now keeps 35PB across 100B pages with crawl-to-search latency under five minutes. Adobe runs 40-50PB across 800K tables from the same format. The source is an investment announcement, and Tunguz's firm led the Series A, so the evidence comes from customer testimonials the author has a financial stake in. The architectural claim is real. The metrics are unverified.

Simon Willison published Datasette Lite on November 17 after a May AI research prototype. The post, documented in `ins_willison-python-browser-service-worker`, describes a service-worker routing pattern that lets any SQLite-backed Python app run fully in the browser. The old approach injected HTML via innerHTML, which broke JavaScript execution and CSS loading. The new approach uses a service worker to intercept all requests and forward them to a web worker holding a persistent Pyodide instance. Because it preserves the standard HTTP request-response contract, all browser behavior works without special handling. Willison states the pattern is not Datasette-specific: "Nothing about how Datasette Lite works is unique to Datasette."

The two examples are not in the same domain, and the authors are not responding to each other. Both describe the same move: one format or one routing layer handles what a collection of specialized tools previously required.

- Tom Tunguz, `ins_tunguz-lakehouse-ai`. A single columnar format with incremental append semantics is replacing separate training and inference data stores at AI lab scale. Source has investment conflict; evidence unverified.
- Simon Willison, `ins_willison-python-browser-service-worker`. Routing all browser requests through a service worker to a persistent Pyodide web worker lets any SQLite-backed Python app run fully client-side.
