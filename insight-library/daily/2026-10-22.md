# What landed today

Four cards, four sources, three lanes. Two cards extend the vocabulary of how people actually work with AI as agents become real. Two more describe defaults that fail silently: one in eval tooling, one in AI pricing. All four are from within the last seven days.

## Work modes evolve as agents become real

Ethan Mollick's October 20 essay, "30 Months on the Exponential (in Charts)," marks the point at which agents changed the shape of human-AI collaboration enough to require a new term. His earlier taxonomy named two modes: centaur (divided work) and cyborg (blended step-by-step co-editing). `ins_mollick-conjurer-oversight-gap` adds a third. Conjurers summon agents, assign work, and evaluate what comes back.

> "Conjurers summon agents when needed, assign them work, and evaluates what comes back."

The conjurer mode's power is scale. The conjurer's risk is opacity: because they do not see each intermediate step, errors accumulate invisibly until the output arrives. Mollick illustrates both sides with his book launch, where a delegated agent caught his math error while also doing things he did not fully see or direct. The conjurer taxonomy connects directly to `ins_mollick-commission-not-steer` and `ins_mollick-delegation-over-prompting`, adding the specific new variable of mid-run invisibility.

Simon Willison's October 19 link post adds a complementary fracture line in `ins_willison-programming-process-loss`. He picks up Jeremy Scheff's "obituary for programming" and adds his own split: the same agentic automation is a gift for programmers who valued outcomes, and a real loss for those who valued the solving itself.

> "If directly solving those problems was a big part of your enjoyment, the loss is very real."

The mechanism is clean: agents remove process reward while preserving outcome reward, and the net effect on any given programmer depends entirely on which reward they were there for. Both cards map onto the same underlying shift: as agents take over more steps, the question of what humans contribute and what they lose becomes personal rather than categorical.

- Ethan Mollick, `ins_mollick-conjurer-oversight-gap`. The conjurer mode extends centaur and cyborg with a third pattern: summon, delegate, evaluate, with oversight gaps that scale with agent autonomy.
- Simon Willison, `ins_willison-programming-process-loss`. Agent automation splits programmers by intrinsic motivation: those who valued output gain, those who valued problem-solving lose.

## Default settings fail silently

Hamel Husain and Antaripa Saha published "How To Use Decision Models for Evals" on October 20. The core finding in `ins_husain-decision-model-threshold-tuning` is blunt: the default 0.50 failure threshold shipped by decision-model APIs is often wrong, and using it without validating it on labeled data can cause a well-calibrated model to catch zero failures.

> "Using this default without checking it against your data can give you subpar results."

The mechanism is precise. A decision model might score all genuine failures at 0.40 and all passing traces at 0.10. It separates the classes perfectly at a 0.30 threshold, but the 0.50 default catches nothing. Husain and Saha tested six models on 98 labeled apartment-leasing traces. Clef Flash's calibration failure was the clearest case: one score group had an average predicted failure probability of about 8%, yet 60% of those traces were actual failures. This is not a theoretical risk; it appeared in a real benchmark comparison.

Elena Verna's October 16 essay, "What on earth is going on with AI pricing and packaging?", applies the same logic to pricing. `ins_verna-ai-pricing-arcade-money` names four compounding forces that make AI costs feel abstract: invented currencies (credits, tokens), large-number framing without a stated baseline, no settled unit of value for general-purpose agents, and unpredictable cost-to-serve that companies hide by avoiding transparent pricing.

> "Some companies promise 5x more usage without explaining 5x of WHAT."

Early adopters tolerate this opacity because novelty compensates. The mass market will not. Verna points to ElevenLabs (audio minutes) and Firecrawl (pages scraped, searches) as companies that have found legible units; the rest of the industry has not. Her conclusion: whoever makes AI costs understandable, predictable, and aligned with customer value gains a structural advantage as adoption widens.

- Hamel Husain, `ins_husain-decision-model-threshold-tuning`. The default 0.50 decision-model threshold needs data-driven tuning; applying it without labeled examples can produce zero recall even on a well-performing model.
- Elena Verna, `ins_verna-ai-pricing-arcade-money`. AI credit pricing behaves like arcade money: invented currencies and undefined units make costs feel unreal, which works with early adopters and breaks with the mass market.
