# What landed today

Three cards from two operators. Ethan Mollick's October 1 essay and Simon Willison's October 14 post. Two lanes: future of work and agentic engineering. One quiet thread connects them: the human's contribution matters more in proportion to what AI can now do on its own.

## What agent swarms changed about the management problem

Ethan Mollick's "The Dot and the Swarm" opens with an admission of being wrong. He had expected agent swarms to require carefully designed human coordination structures. They did not. `ins_mollick-swarm-direction-bottleneck` captures the reframe. Explicit management scaffolding (step-by-step chains, fixed routing, role assignments) keeps getting outperformed by better models that self-organize. The human effort that used to go into designing agent topology now has diminishing returns. What has rising returns is deciding what goals to give the swarm in the first place.

The mechanism is the absence, not the presence, of something. Most management overhead exists to solve human problems: misaligned incentives, information hoarding, communication friction across hierarchies. Agent teams do not have these problems. They share context directly and have no incentive misalignment with peers. Coordination infrastructure drops out, not because someone engineered a clever routing layer, but because the underlying cause of coordination overhead is missing.

Mollick cites a reported case of thousands of agents producing a Navier-Stokes proof in 88 hours, with approximately 2.7 million messages exchanged and what he describes as a "remarkably thin" human coordination structure. He frames this as the Bitter Lesson applied to agent organization: computation beats human scaffolding here too.

- Ethan Mollick, `ins_mollick-swarm-direction-bottleneck`. Direction quality is the binding constraint once agents can self-coordinate; coordination scaffolding yields diminishing returns as models improve.

## What proactive agents catch that chatbots cannot

The second card from the same essay sits one level closer to the user. `ins_mollick-proactive-agents-caught-not-asked` documents a behavioral shift in how people describe their AI. Users of proactive personal agents (Mollick calls them "Clawlikes," including Meta's Muse and tools like Dot) increasingly tell stories about what their AI did rather than what they asked it to do. A wrong project number caught in a permit email. An airline credit extension requested before the window closed. The value proposition differs from a chatbot answer: the agent caught something the user was not tracking.

The mechanism is the boundary of what the user knows to ask. A chatbot is bounded by that. A proactive agent monitoring accounts and external documents is not. When the catch happens, users describe the experience emotionally, as something that made them feel good, not merely satisfied. That signal is different from the satisfaction profile of a correctly answered question, and it predicts a different relationship with the tool.

- Ethan Mollick, `ins_mollick-proactive-agents-caught-not-asked`. Proactive personal agents create value bounded by what accounts reveal, not by what users think to ask.

## A quality goal in the prompt changes what the model optimizes for

Simon Willison's October 14 post is a brief experiment with a specific technique. He prompted Claude Opus 5.5 Max through the iPhone chat interface to build a Three.js game with trees, adding explicit instruction to make it "both fun and beautiful." The model ran for 3 hours and 7 minutes and produced a spot-the-difference game with procedurally generated trees, drag-and-zoom exploration, music, animations, achievements, appreciation points, and a herbarium mechanic. His summary of the explicit fun instruction: it works "uncomfortably well."

`ins_willison-quality-goal-prompt` draws out the mechanism. An LLM generating code defaults to functional correctness as the implicit goal. A stated quality criterion adds a second evaluation axis that the model applies at each decision point in unspecified degrees of freedom. Without it, the model satisfies the spec. With it, the model satisfies the spec while also optimizing for the stated attribute throughout generation. The animation, the herbarium, the achievement system: none were in the functional spec. All arrived because the model had an explicit instruction about what "good" meant.

- Simon Willison, `ins_willison-quality-goal-prompt`. Adding an explicit quality goal to a creative coding prompt produces polish in unspecified degrees of freedom that a functional spec alone does not.
