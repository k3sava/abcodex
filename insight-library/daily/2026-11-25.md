# What landed today

Five cards, no new operators. Three themes cut across the week's output from different lanes. Kevin Indig and Simon Willison both published measurement arguments: Indig on how engine changes corrupt AI visibility metrics, Willison on what the second LLM inflection point actually changed in practice. Ethan Mollick and Dan Shipper arrived at the same gap from different directions: frontier AI fails at self-evaluation, and that failure is exactly why human judgment stays scarce. Chris Zukowski's game marketing post rounds out the day with a structural claim about what makes a game hard to replicate.

## Theme 1, Measuring AI correctly requires tracking share, not counts

Kevin Indig published "DenominAItor" on November 23, coining a term for the total citations an AI engine hands out per answer. His central claim, documented in `ins_indig-citation-share-not-raw-count`, is that raw citation counts mislead because model updates inflate or deflate the denominator without changing your actual standing. When ChatGPT's August 2026 update caused engines to serve 51% more cited URLs per prompt, every brand's raw count rose. No one improved their content. The engine changed.

His fix is to track share: your cited URLs as a percentage of all cited URLs in the same answers. Share rises only if you outperform the pool. He also finds that citations and referral traffic were negatively correlated across all three major engines, meaning citation counts rose while actual traffic fell on Perplexity. His framing for the underlying dynamic is blunt: "model releases are the new algorithm updates."

The measurement failure Indig describes is the AI-search equivalent of a claim he made in June 2026, documented in `ins_indig-algorithm-visibility-rental`: algorithm-gifted visibility is a rental. The engine giveth and the engine taketh away. The DenominAItor argument extends that logic to citation counts.

- Kevin Indig, `ins_indig-citation-share-not-raw-count`. Raw AI citation counts mislead when engine updates inflate the denominator; track citation share against the total pool in the same answers.

## Theme 2, The second LLM inflection point changed what can be delegated

Simon Willison gave a keynote at the AI Engineer Code Summit on November 24, naming three LLM inflection points from the past twelve months. The first, November 2025 coding agents, is already in the library as `ins_november-2025-coding-inflection`. The second is new.

`ins_willison-fable-class-proactive-tools` documents the June and July 2026 shift: Fable-class models solve new problems by building the tools they need rather than stopping at the limits of their existing toolset. When debugging a Safari display glitch, Claude Fable built its own screenshot tool and logging server without being asked. Given five microphones and a task, a Fable-class model located a beep to within 40 centimeters. The model identified what it needed and created it. This removes the ceiling that previously required human engineering before complex tasks could be delegated.

Willison also names a third inflection: September and October 2026, when frontier models became strong at 3D modeling and game design evaluation, spending 12 hours in autonomous iterative playtesting to rebalance a game from dull to engaging. He is undecided whether Fable 5.2 is a fourth inflection or an increment.

The second inflection point is the most structurally significant for operators: the constraint is no longer "build the right tooling first." The model builds it.

- Simon Willison, `ins_willison-fable-class-proactive-tools`. Fable-class models construct the tools a task requires rather than stopping when pre-supplied tools run out.

## Theme 3, The judgment gap: why AI fails at self-evaluation and why that keeps human ranking scarce

Ethan Mollick and Dan Shipper published independently on the same week and arrived at the same structural gap from different directions.

Mollick's November 9 essay, "Fable 5.2 and Jagged AGI," ran a series of deep experiments across hardware design, business planning, humanities teaching, economics research, and 3D creative building. The model performed at or near expert breadth across all of them. The failure was consistent and specific. `ins_mollick-agi-self-evaluation-gap` captures it: frontier AI fails at self-evaluation, not at task breadth. The model uses critics, judges, and fact-checkers that are optimized to catch factual errors. Those loops do not ask whether the output is too long, repetitive, or misses what was actually wanted. A 182-page research paper can be factually accurate and still fail. A 204-section rulebook can be internally consistent and still defeat its own purpose. Mollick's steering supplied the missing question: "Is this what I wanted?"

Dan Shipper's November 15 essay reaches the structural implication from the builder side. `ins_shipper-judgment-scarce-generation-cheap` documents his core claim: when everyone has the same AI models, generation capacity equalizes, and the scarce resource becomes ranking. His concrete test: Every's team ran the same customer survey through Claude, and each person drew different insights. The model generated the candidates; the person with accountability for a specific area decided which mattered. His phrase for what makes ranking hard is "a non-consensus perspective": a view shaped by deep context, responsibility, and history with the problem.

Together, Mollick and Shipper name the same gap from opposite sides. Mollick shows what frontier AI cannot do from the inside (self-evaluate against intent). Shipper shows why human judgment stays valuable on the outside (you hold the context the model cannot infer). The gap is the same: the question "is this actually good?" requires someone with skin in the game.

- Ethan Mollick, `ins_mollick-agi-self-evaluation-gap`. Frontier AI verifies factual accuracy but does not notice when output is too long, repetitive, or misses the point; human steering supplies the missing loop.
- Dan Shipper, `ins_shipper-judgment-scarce-generation-cheap`. When AI makes generation cheap, the scarce resource shifts to ranking and judgment about what matters, rooted in accountability for a specific area.
- Chris Zukowski, `ins_zukowski-emergent-appeal-blocks-cloning`. Games become structurally hard to clone when their core appeal requires hours of play to understand, because cloners cannot reverse-engineer what public signals cannot convey.
