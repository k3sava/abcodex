# What landed today

Three cards. Two are from October 19, one from October 18. Two themes connected across independent operators this week: how AI reshapes work at the individual level, and why measurement assumptions built for a pre-AI analytics landscape are failing.

## Work changes shape as agents arrive

Simon Willison and Ethan Mollick published on consecutive days and reached complementary conclusions about what AI does to professional work, neither citing the other.

Willison's October 19 link post on Jeremy Scheff's essay "My obituary for programming" (`ins_willison-programming-splits-system-vs-puzzle`) makes a clean structural argument: programming always bundled two activities, solving novel logic problems and assembling those solutions into working systems. Non-programmers couldn't do either, so both were paid. LLMs can now competently handle the logic-puzzle layer. The scarcity premium on puzzle-solving is gone for most routine problems. What remains, and what agents cannot replace, is the judgment work: directing agents, composing components, owning production, and deciding what to build. Willison's own words mark the stakes clearly:

> "If you're someone who thrives on building working systems and doesn't care too deeply about the problems solved along the way, LLMs are the gift that keeps on giving."

> "If directly solving those problems was a big part of your enjoyment, the loss is very real."

Mollick's October 18 Charter interview (`ins_mollick-ai-single-player-org-gap`) takes the same logic one level up: from individual skill redistribution to organizational performance gaps. His argument is that AI tools are "single player, not multiplayer." Chatbots improve individual throughput in the session where they run. They do not create the shared processes, common context, and mutual adjustment that group performance requires. Organizations that treat AI as an IT rollout, expecting individual gains to aggregate automatically, fall into what Mollick calls "a trap." His evidence: a used-car negotiation study found that individual skill in directing AI explained 73% of outcome variation. Those individual differences do not combine cleanly at the group level without deliberate work redesign.

The two cards have different primary operators, different source types, and different specific claims. The convergence point: agents redistribute work within a role or organization rather than eliminating it, and the redistribution is sharper and more consequential than most teams currently assume.

- Simon Willison, `ins_willison-programming-splits-system-vs-puzzle`. LLMs remove the scarcity premium on logic-puzzle-solving in programming, shifting value toward system-builders who direct agents rather than write logic.
- Ethan Mollick, `ins_mollick-ai-single-player-org-gap`. AI productivity gains are session-level and individual; group-level gains require leaders to redesign work structures, not just deploy tools.

## AI search attribution requires new measurement discipline

Kevin Indig's October 19 Growth Memo analysis (`ins_indig-ai-direct-traffic-unverifiable`) is methodologically rigorous and points to a measurement failure that is likely affecting many brands.

The hypothesis going into his analysis was intuitive: AI Mode citations don't always pass UTM referral data, so AI-driven visitors arrive in GA4 as Direct. Higher AI visibility should therefore show up as higher Direct channel growth. His analysis across 12 sites and 4 brands, running 632 correlation tests over 8 months, found no reliable link. The core problem: only 0.003% of Direct sessions contained any identifiable AI trace. Bot traffic in the Direct channel is large enough that any genuine AI-to-Direct signal is lost inside it. Eight of twelve sites showed Direct growth from August to September, but the growth was predominantly bot-driven.

The practical implication is that brands asking "is AI search bringing us more visitors?" cannot answer that question by looking at Direct traffic growth. Indig proposes three-signal triangulation instead: brand mention tracking across AI surfaces to establish visibility, self-reported attribution surveys to capture stated intent, and business outcome connection to test whether AI-channel activity maps to conversion or revenue.

This card sits alongside the earlier `ins_indig-ai-mode-shortlist-acceptance` finding (AI Mode accepts 88% of shortlisted sources). That card established that AI search visibility is real and measurable. This one establishes that the revenue impact of that visibility is not yet measurable through standard web analytics, and names the method to close that gap.

- Kevin Indig, `ins_indig-ai-direct-traffic-unverifiable`. GA4 correlation analysis cannot isolate AI search's contribution to Direct traffic because bot inflation swamps the signal; three-signal triangulation is required instead.
