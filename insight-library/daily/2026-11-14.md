# What landed today

Five cards, four new operators. Every's Thesis: 2027 conference on November 11 concentrated operator thinking on a single question: when AI can generate plausible outputs for most knowledge tasks, what stays human? Dan Shipper delivered the mechanism. The other four operators — Ivan Zhao, Curtis Anderson, Josh Miller, and Katy George — described what that answer implies for different parts of the stack.

## Theme 1, Ranking is now the scarce skill

Shipper ran an internal test at the conference. His head of evals fed his Slack messages to Claude and asked it to predict his replies. Claude hit 93% recall on generation across 400 candidates. It could not reliably rank them. The test made the mechanism visible.

`ins_shipper-ranking-scarce-generation-cheap` captures what follows: the value is not in generating more outputs. Everyone has access to that. The value is in knowing which outputs are good for your specific situation, and that judgment comes from a personal perspective the model cannot replicate on your behalf.

> "Generation is really cheap, but ranking is hard."

> "What's actually expensive and rare is how you use them, through your perspective."

Shipper used one Claude subscription in a week to do work he estimated would have required five specialists in 2020. The bottleneck was not access to generation. It was knowing what good looked like for Every, specifically.

- Dan Shipper, `ins_shipper-ranking-scarce-generation-cheap`. When generation is cheap, the scarce skill is ranking outputs using personal perspective.

## Theme 2, What the human role becomes

The four adjacent operators at the same conference each described a different implication of Shipper's mechanism.

Ivan Zhao's answer is about demand. Even if AI can produce equivalent creative outputs, audiences may still prefer human-made work because they consume it as an expression of another person — the same reason they still watch human athletes play sports rather than AI simulations. `ins_zhao-human-token-preference` frames this as the market for "human tokens": work people want because another person made it.

> "We like to watch other meatball[s] play chess, not silicon play chess."

Katy George's answer is about roles. When AI handles execution, the high-value job becomes teaching AI to do your work and raising the bar on what it produces. `ins_george-teaching-ai-raising-bar` documents the shift: "not doing stuff ourselves, but actually teaching AI to do it." The scarce skill is discernment, not output.

Josh Miller's answer is about method. `ins_miller-ai-prompter-not-delegatee` captures his position: AI works better as a Socratic system that surfaces your own thinking than as a delegatee. The person who delegated doesn't understand their own output. The person who was prompted does.

> "What if AI prompts you so you can come up with the right answer?"

Curtis Anderson's answer is about hiring. `ins_anderson-ambiguity-near-death-screen` documents his interview practice at Nursa: he screens for candidates who have survived genuinely ambiguous situations, asking directly how many near-death experiences they have had. The logic follows from Shipper's mechanism — AI handles the well-defined tasks; what remains requires judgment in ambiguity. Prior exposure to ambiguity is the signal for that.

- Ivan Zhao, `ins_zhao-human-token-preference`. Audiences prefer human-made work because they consume it as another person's expression, not just the content.
- Katy George, `ins_george-teaching-ai-raising-bar`. The emerging high-value role is teaching AI to do your work and raising the standard it must meet.
- Josh Miller, `ins_miller-ai-prompter-not-delegatee`. AI as a Socratic prompter preserves human agency; AI as a delegatee erodes it.
- Curtis Anderson, `ins_anderson-ambiguity-near-death-screen`. Screening for prior near-death professional experiences predicts AI-era performance better than conventional interviews.
