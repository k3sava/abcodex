# What landed today

Three cards, no new operators. Two distinct clusters. Tom Tunguz uses fresh Databricks data to map the enterprise AI market's pricing tier split. The AIE Code Summit 2026, running November 10-12 in San Francisco, produced two more: a community manifesto on output quality and a Dex Horthy talk on what happens when teams accelerate only half of their software factory.

## Theme 1, Enterprise AI is bifurcating into two markets with different leaders

Tunguz's Nov 16 post is the clearest empirical account yet of how the enterprise AI market has split by quality tier. `ins_tunguz-frontier-tier-structural-vs-strategic` documents what Databricks Unity Gateway telemetry shows across its enterprise customers: Anthropic holds 94% of frontier-tier token volume, up from 81% before Opus 5.5 launched on Sep 21. OpenAI holds 81% of low-end volume after cutting prices roughly 90% across two rounds (80% cut July 30, then another 50%). These are not the same market. They have different buyers, different value drivers, and different competitive dynamics.

The question Tunguz raises but cannot answer from the data alone is whether OpenAI's low-end share reflects a structural cost advantage (GPU kernel improvements, speculative decoding) or a deliberate price subsidy it can't sustain. That distinction matters for anyone pricing enterprise AI products. A structural advantage means OpenAI can hold that position permanently and profitably. A deliberate subsidy means pricing pressure in the low-end tier will eventually ease. Neither Anthropic nor OpenAI has disclosed which it is.

- Tom Tunguz, `ins_tunguz-frontier-tier-structural-vs-strategic`. Databricks production data shows the enterprise AI market has bifurcated: Anthropic owns frontier-tier volume on model quality, OpenAI owns low-end volume on price cuts.

## Theme 2, The AIE Code Summit produced two convergent cards on the same bottleneck

The No AI Slop Manifesto and Dex Horthy's talk arrived from the same event, and they name the same constraint from two angles.

The manifesto, documented in `ins_swyx-slop-output-review-asymmetry`, was introduced at the AIE Code Summit by Shawn Wang, Philip Kiely, Lucas Meijer, and Kent C. Dodds, with 352 practitioners signing on. Its core claim is structural: producing AI output now costs nearly zero, but reviewing it costs the same as before. Teams optimizing for token throughput without updating review culture create a compounding loop. Agents read and imitate existing repository patterns. One low-quality decision becomes precedent, then convention, then architectural debt that the next agent faithfully copies.

> "Slop is when we put less effort into producing something than we ask the consumer to spend understanding it."

The manifesto's resolution is accountability at the sender: whoever passes along AI output is responsible for it. When a mistake recurs, build a linter or automated check. Don't file another warning document.

Horthy's talk at the same summit, documented in `ins_horthy-factory-delivery-pressure`, frames the constraint at the system level rather than the per-output level. His talk title was "A software factory needs pressure in both directions." The claim from the event program: "Replacing the builder doesn't make the rest of the delivery system optional." Horthy's prior work at HumanLayer described how long-running agent runs compound trajectory errors inside a single session. The AIE Code Summit talk appears to extend that to the factory level: a team that installs AI agents on the generation side and leaves the review, testing, and deployment infrastructure unchanged has not made a faster factory. It has made a bigger backlog.

The two cards update `pat_ai-code-shifts-quality-bottleneck` from 3 operators to 6, adding the Nov 15 Sivasubramanian card and both summit cards. They also extend `pat_verification-as-human-job` from 15 operators to 17.

- Shawn Wang, `ins_swyx-slop-output-review-asymmetry`. AI generation is now nearly free; reviewing AI output costs as much as before. Slop compounds because agents imitate existing patterns, including the bad ones.
- Dex Horthy, `ins_horthy-factory-delivery-pressure`. Speeding the builder side of a software factory stalls delivery at the verification node. Pressure must run in both directions.
