# What landed today

Four cards from three operators across two lanes. Two themes emerged from independent angles: how far agents can extend their reach in production, and where human judgment remains the resource that agents cannot replace.

## Theme 1, Where agents can go in production

Thorsten Ball and Simon Willison each published new boundary-testing work on Nov 8 and 9. `ins_ball-agent-full-sdlc-db-boundary` captures Ball's description of Amp's current agent scope: agents ship to production, monitor rollouts, read logs, toggle feature flags, send emails, grant credits, and investigate database issues. The one exclusion is writing to the database. His reasoning is not caution for caution's sake. As he writes in Joy & Curiosity #105:

> "The models have become too good to keep them boxed in as smart typewriters."

The boundary is irreversibility, not visibility. Code changes can be rolled back through feature flags; database writes cannot. This is the clearest statement yet from an operator who has actually drawn the line.

Willison's beep locator project, `ins_willison-sensory-channel-coding-agent`, extends that reach in a different direction. Claude has no native audio input. It can, however, run JavaScript and Python that capture and analyze microphone data from any browser-capable device. The agent Willison built used browser-based listeners, a Python coordination server, and hot-swapped JavaScript over WebSocket, all written by Claude during the session. The system located a mystery beep source to within 40 cm, which turned out to be an old carbon monoxide detector. As Willison puts it: "Figuring out ways to let coding agents perceive the world around them is a whole lot of fun."

- Thorsten Ball, `ins_ball-agent-full-sdlc-db-boundary`. Agents at Amp own every SDLC step except database writes; irreversibility is the trust boundary, not code visibility.
- Simon Willison, `ins_willison-sensory-channel-coding-agent`. A coding agent extended with browser-based microphone listeners and a local server located a physical sound source to within 40 cm, demonstrating that agent capability scales with what can be instrumented.

## Theme 2, Where human judgment still carries the signal

Two cards this week converge on the same underlying pattern: the places where human judgment matters most are not where AI is weakest at facts, but where AI cannot evaluate the quality of its own output.

Ethan Mollick tested Fable 5.2 on open-ended, long-form tasks in "Fable 5.2 and Jagged AGI." `ins_mollick-jagged-agi-self-eval-gap` captures his finding that the model's failures were not factual errors. They were proportion and taste failures: a business plan that ran 182 pages, a game rulebook that expanded to 87,500 words without the model asking whether that was too long. Mollick calls this the new "jagged" frontier:

> "Knowing when your own work is not good enough is a large part of what makes someone an expert."

Models build critics and fact-checkers but do not build editors. The gap is not in the generation but in the evaluation of the generation.

Willison's details-summary PR technique, `ins_willison-pr-human-opener-ai-collapsed`, is the applied version of the same insight at the code-review level. AI-generated PR descriptions are accurate and dense. Reviewers see a wall of text and skip it. Willison's fix: a brief human-written opener precedes the full AI description, which is collapsed in an HTML `<details><summary>` block. "My promise to the reader is that the short opener will represent my human judgement." The human editorial signal is what gives the AI output its credibility.

- Ethan Mollick, `ins_mollick-jagged-agi-self-eval-gap`. Frontier models now fail on self-evaluation: they produce accurate but over-long, repetitive output because they cannot judge the quality of their own work.
- Simon Willison, `ins_willison-pr-human-opener-ai-collapsed`. Placing a brief human-written opener before a collapsed AI PR description ensures reviewers get a human-curated signal first without discarding detail.
