# What landed today

Four cards, two operators. Tom Tunguz published a single essay that yields three distinct, non-overlapping claims about AI stack economics. Simon Willison pulled the thread on Census Bureau adoption data and found the measurement baseline changed twice. Two themes: the structural economics of building AI products, and the reliability of the data people cite to justify building them.

## Theme 1, The harness layer controls margin, and the application layer above it is fragile

Tom Tunguz asked four questions about the AI stack in a November 2 essay. Three of those questions produce cards in this run.

`ins_tunguz-harness-layer-margin-engine` is the sharpest finding. Input context makes up as much as 98% of tokens in a typical AI request. That means the harness, the code that assembles what goes into a prompt, controls the largest cost line. Tunguz reports a 71% cost reduction and gross margin improvement from 38% to 75% from harness redesign alone, with no model change. The fight is not over which model you pick. It is over who writes the context assembly code.

> "the fight moves to who owns the harness"

`ins_tunguz-thin-wrapper-feature-absorption` is the corollary for the layer above. If the harness is where margin lives, the application layer is where margin dies. A product built on a single narrow AI capability has no moat beyond the gap in the base model. Once the model closes the gap, the product loses its reason to exist.

> "Thin wrappers die when the model absorbs their feature."

`ins_tunguz-ai-stack-double-pendulum` ties the three cards together as a system. Tunguz names four stack layers, each asking its own question, and notes that shifts in any one propagate unpredictably through the others. The double pendulum metaphor holds: two simple connected arms, chaotic motion. A model capability jump changes what the harness must do, which changes which application features are defensible. CoreWeave credit default swaps trading at 1,090 basis points over Treasuries signals that the datacenter layer, the fourth question, has no comfortable answer yet either.

- Tom Tunguz, `ins_tunguz-harness-layer-margin-engine`. Input context dominates token cost, making harness-layer design the primary gross margin lever.
- Tom Tunguz, `ins_tunguz-thin-wrapper-feature-absorption`. Application-layer thin wrappers lose differentiation when the base model absorbs their defining feature.
- Tom Tunguz, `ins_tunguz-ai-stack-double-pendulum`. The four-layer AI stack couples like a double pendulum, making single-layer investment theses unreliable.

## Theme 2, The AI adoption data people cite broke twice in twelve months

`ins_willison-ai-survey-methodology-breaks` records something that will take time to surface in broader discourse. The Census Bureau's Business Trends and Outlook Survey changed its AI adoption question twice since late 2025. First, a Nov 2025 change broadened scope from using AI "in producing goods or services" to "any of its business functions." The Bureau noted those periods were incomparable. Then EO 14434 renamed "AI" to "Super Intelligence or SI, formerly referred to as Artificial Intelligence or AI" in October 2026.

> "Beginning with the October 19, 2026 collection, instrument wording was updated due to Executive Order 14434."

Large-firm adoption figures across the three incompatible periods: roughly 12%, then roughly 30%, then 40.7%. Each jump reflects a different thing being measured. Willison's fix is methodologically clean: separate lines per wording period, 6-survey trailing average within each. The directional signal may still hold, but anyone citing the headline number as a continuous trend is comparing figures that do not compare.

- Simon Willison, `ins_willison-ai-survey-methodology-breaks`. The headline U.S. AI adoption data reflects three incompatible question wordings, making cross-period trend comparisons unreliable.
