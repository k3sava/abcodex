# What landed today

Four cards, one new operator. Two themes from three operators today: how context failure modes in agents extend beyond fixation; and how AI search behavior data is challenging the measurement models that media and search teams inherited from Google.

## Theme 1, Chat history is an implicit instruction layer and it overrides explicit instructions

Drew Breunig's "State of Context Engineering" essay from October 24 named five distinct context failure modes. Two were captured in the previous run. Today's card adds context distraction, the one with the most direct evidence behind it.

`ins_breunig-chat-history-overrides-instructions` anchors to the Camassa and Shiller "Do as I Say, Not as I Do" experiment run across 13 models. The finding is concrete: a model following an explicit instruction begins drifting from it after a few exchanges that modeled a different behavior. Gemma 3 12B followed the explicit instruction less than half the time after only a few contradictory examples. Even Opus 4.6 eventually shifted.

> "Those examples start to influence the model."

The mechanism is not a bug but a feature misapplied. Models treat context as demonstration. Past exchanges function as few-shot examples even when the user did not intend them as such. The practical implication: long-running sessions and memory features that accumulate conversation history carry mounting implicit instruction weight that can outpull explicit directives.

This card connects to two already in the library. `ins_breunig-context-inertia-agent-fixation` (from October 14) is about agents persisting on a bad hypothesis. Context distraction is different: it is about behavior drift from instruction, not hypothesis lock-in. `ins_breunig-long-context-error-amplification` covers the broader point that larger context windows admit more errors. Context distraction is the specific mechanism by which those errors reach the model's behavior.

- Drew Breunig, `ins_breunig-chat-history-overrides-instructions`. Contradictory examples accumulated in chat history override explicit instructions because models read past exchanges as implicit demonstrations, not just context.

## Theme 2, AI search and Google serve different cognitive moments, not the same one with different interfaces

The two Dombrowski cards and the King card connect across two iPullRank articles and point in the same direction: the measurement and strategy frameworks inherited from Google do not apply to AI search because the surfaces serve different cognitive jobs.

Cate Dombrowski matched 16,310 users at the user level across Google and AI clickstream panels. `ins_dombrowski-ai-google-query-moment-split` reports the headline finding: 96% of AI prompts had no Google counterpart within 30 minutes. The same user is not choosing AI over Google for the same query. They are reaching for different tools for different moments. A user's Google habits explained only 4% of their AI behavior.

`ins_dombrowski-surface-shapes-query-expression` adds the behavioral texture. The same user phrases the same need in 9 words on AI and 5 words on Google. Questions are 27% of AI prompts and 8% of Google queries. Personal context appears in 8% of AI prompts and 1.2% of Google queries. This is not a preference difference. It is a conditioned reflex difference. Google trained users to compress needs into keywords. AI surfaces reward richer expression. The conditioning breaks when the rewarded behavior changes.

> "Google trained all of us to compress a need into a few nouns."

Mike King's `ins_king-ai-search-branding-not-traffic` takes this a step further on the measurement side. The studies finding low AI search traffic (ChatGPT delivering 0.62% of Google organic traffic, per Marie Haynes; organic delivering 39x AI traffic per SEO Works) are real. His argument is not that the data is wrong. It is that the metric is wrong.

> "every one of these studies grades a brand channel with a traffic ruler."

> "An AI answer is earned media composed by a machine."

Content in an AI answer is not competing for a click. The system composes the answer and the content is raw material. The return is presence and attribution, not a visit. One client site showed a 0.72 correlation between AI share of voice and direct traffic at a three-week lag. This data point supports the brand-effect model but it is one site, and the three-week lag figure needs broader replication.

The three cards together map a coherent picture: AI and Google handle different cognitive moments (Dombrowski sessions data), users express themselves differently on each surface because the surface conditions expression (Dombrowski query data), and measuring AI search by traffic applies Google metrics to a channel that operates by brand logic (King reframe).

- Cate Dombrowski, `ins_dombrowski-ai-google-query-moment-split`. Matched-panel data on 16,310 users finds 96% of AI prompts had no Google counterpart in the same session, separating AI and Google by cognitive moment rather than by surface preference.
- Cate Dombrowski, `ins_dombrowski-surface-shapes-query-expression`. The same users phrase needs as 9-word questions on AI and 5-word keywords on Google, because each surface rewards different expression patterns.
- Mike King, `ins_king-ai-search-branding-not-traffic`. Low AI referral traffic reflects a measurement category error: AI answers are earned media composed by machines, and the return is brand presence and attribution, not clicks.
