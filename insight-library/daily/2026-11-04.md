# What landed today

Four cards, four operators. Tom Tunguz turned Anthropic's S-1 amendment into a card on the financial mechanic that will separate AI lab winners from losers in 2027. Kevin Indig published two-year channel data showing email growing while organic fell. Ethan Mollick put a number on the Conjurer delegation trap. Kyle Kingsbury, writing after testing ParticleDB with Jepsen, put the documentation credentialing problem plainly. Two structural themes connect across the four: the economics of what costs and what compounds when AI is in the stack, and the signals that people use to assess quality when AI makes proxy signals cheap to fake.

## Theme 1, The economics of AI: burst compute, owned channels, and the trap in delegation

`ins_tunguz-ai-lab-compute-duration-mismatch` is the sharpest finding of the day. Tunguz analyzed Anthropic's S-1/A and found that short-term burst GPU rental has risen from roughly $3 to $12 to $13 per hour. Blended GPU cost rose 82% over two quarters. In Q3 2026, Anthropic spent $5.0 billion on burst compute, 51% of a $9.86 billion quarterly compute bill. A $974 million Q2 operating profit became a $567 million loss in Q3. The fix is not more efficient models alone. It is matching customer contract duration to GPU lease duration.

> "Financial engineering in 2027 means closing that duration mismatch."

Labs that lock enterprise buyers into multi-year committed contracts hedge burst exposure. Labs that keep mostly monthly or pay-as-you-go customers absorb the full cost of every demand spike. This is now a revenue-operations problem as much as an infrastructure one.

- Tom Tunguz, `ins_tunguz-ai-lab-compute-duration-mismatch`. GPU burst costs have doubled in two quarters; the margin fix is duration matching between compute leases and customer contracts.

`ins_indig-email-owned-channel-floor` runs a parallel argument at the channel layer below the AI lab stack. Indig published Similarweb data showing email up 16.3% in the US and 17.0% in France year over year while organic search fell 13% US and 24% France over two years. The mechanism is ownership. Organic traffic flows through an algorithm the site does not control. Email subscribers stay on the list regardless of algorithm changes. Indig on the asymmetry:

> "I see email as severely underrated and a prime audience-building channel."

The owned-vs-rented framing generalizes: any channel where platform changes can erase traffic overnight is structurally fragile. Email is not glamorous. It compounds.

- Kevin Indig, `ins_indig-email-owned-channel-floor`. Email held and grew while organic search fell 13 to 24% because subscriber relationships are owned; algorithm changes do not affect them.

`ins_mollick-conjurer-verification-trap` closes the economic theme with the delegation arithmetic. Conjurer-mode AI delegation has two cost components: AI doing time (near zero for the user) and human verification time (non-zero). A one-hour task leaves one hour to gain. If reviewing the output takes 30 minutes, the net saving is 30 minutes. For tasks where errors hide in plausible prose, verification costs approach the original doing cost. The Wharton@Work piece attributed to Mollick names this directly: "A one-hour task that takes half an hour to verify is a trap." Three variables govern whether Conjurer mode pays: how long the task takes, how likely the AI is to succeed, and how long checking takes. The trap closes when verification costs are high relative to the task.

- Ethan Mollick, `ins_mollick-conjurer-verification-trap`. Conjurer delegation saves time only when verification costs less than doing; for complex or hard-to-review tasks, the savings shrink or vanish.

## Theme 2, Trust signal erosion when AI makes quality proxies cheap to fake

`ins_kingsbury-llm-docs-trust-erosion` is the fourth card and stands apart from the economics theme. Kyle Kingsbury published a Jepsen analysis of ParticleDB 1.1.4 on October 31. During testing, Jepsen found that LLM tooling repeatedly claimed bugs had been fixed when they had not been. That observation grounds a broader claim about documentation as a trust mechanism.

> "More broadly, the use of LLMs erodes trust in documentation, in software, and in the organizations which produce them."

Polished documentation was costly to produce before AI, so its existence functioned as a credible signal of team investment. When AI can produce polished docs cheaply, documentation quality decouples from code quality. The same tooling that makes docs look mature can generate confident text about correctness that does not exist. Kingsbury does not resolve what should replace documentation as a trust signal. That is the open question the card carries forward.

- Kyle Kingsbury, `ins_kingsbury-llm-docs-trust-erosion`. LLM tooling generates plausible but false assertions about software correctness, breaking documentation quality as a proxy for software reliability.
