# A small software team can run on one weekly meeting when it includes a silent period for writing ambitious shipping goals before anyone states them aloud

## Claim
One scheduled weekly meeting is enough for a small software team to stay aligned, provided the meeting includes a two-to-three minute silent period in which each person writes down ambitious weekly shipping goals before stating them aloud.

## Mechanism
The silent writing step forces each person to crystallize what they intend to ship before they hear what others are committing to. Without it, goals emerge reactively: shaped by what teammates just said, by social pressure to match perceived ambition, or by a natural tendency to stay vague. Writing before speaking moves goal-formation inside the individual before it becomes social. Ball's constraint on ambition is explicit:

> "Don't write down things you check off a few hours later."

The meeting's other sections, Leave (absences), Talk (open discussion on any topic, run loose at roughly 45 minutes), and News Posts (review of public releases and their timing), handle coordination and information flow. The silent goal-writing is the specific mechanism that creates personal accountability rather than group negotiation.

## Conditions
Holds when: the team is small enough that everyone's work is visible in a shared document and the meeting duration stays under two hours. Ball describes a team where every member's goals fit on a single screen.

Fails when: the team is large enough that individual shipping goals cannot be meaningfully reviewed in a 45-minute discussion. Also fails when goals are externally set by quarterly planning rather than weekly commitment, removing individual ownership of scope.

## Evidence
Ball describes Amp's weekly routine in Register Spill #106 (November 15, 2026). The structure has been stable long enough for him to describe it as "a sweet spot." The four sections are fixed: Leave, Talk, Shipping, and News Posts. He runs the meeting with his co-lead Quinn. Each person states goals after the silent writing period.

His framing on process overhead:

> "The goal isn't to follow The Process. Many roads lead to alignment."

On focus during the meeting:

> "It's important that everyone on the call has that document open and is fully focused on it."

He also describes an onboarding filter at Amp: "the first task on the Amp team is to figure out what your first task should be." The meeting structure reflects the same principle: a minimal container that leaves initiative to individuals.

## Signals
- Teams running this structure reporting they cancel fewer ad-hoc syncs during the week
- Goal statements in the weekly meeting that extend beyond what the person could finish in a morning

## Counter-evidence
The structure is designed for Amp's specific context: a small, senior team where everyone already knows the codebase and business priorities. The onboarding filter, asking new hires to identify their own first task, screens for self-direction before the meeting structure can work. Teams with higher onboarding load, less shared context, or more externally constrained work may find the loose Talk section insufficient for real alignment. One newsletter entry is a thin evidence base for a general team-process claim.
