# Executives mandate AI adoption while using it minimally themselves; documented hour tallies beat opinion when managing upward

## Claim
Most senior executives who mandate AI adoption use AI less than one hour a week. Teams that track and share concrete AI usage data rather than arguing on principle navigate these mandates more effectively.

## Mechanism
Executives issuing AI adoption mandates often derive their confidence from general market narratives rather than personal use. When they have little hands-on experience, they lack the grounding to evaluate team resistance, identify false acceleration claims, or distinguish productive adoption from compliance theater. The asymmetry creates a specific communication problem: the team has ground-truth evidence and the executive has general conviction. Sharing usage logs, time-saved tallies, and concrete before/after comparisons shifts the conversation from opinion to data, which executives can engage with directly. Documented hours also make AI overhead visible, countering the framing that AI is purely additive.

## Conditions
Holds when: the executive issuing the mandate has limited personal AI use and bases their position on market signals rather than direct experience. Also holds when the team has been tracking usage data and can surface it concretely.
Fails when: the executive is a heavy AI user themselves, in which case they already have grounding and the data-vs-opinion asymmetry does not apply. Also fails when no tracking infrastructure exists and the team can only argue from anecdote.

## Evidence
Indig and Johnson surveyed senior executives directly to ground this piece. The usage gap is not inferred; it is measured:

69% of senior executives report using AI less than one hour a week, according to survey data cited in the piece. Their prescription for managing upward through this gap is documentation over debate.

## Signals
- Executives respond more constructively to time-tracking dashboards than to team push-back about AI tool quality.
- Visible logs of AI hours, including overhead hours (prompting, reviewing, correcting), shift the framing from "are you using AI?" to "what are we actually getting for it?"
- Teams that document before-and-after task times build more durable relationships with AI-mandating executives than teams that comply silently or resist vocally.

## Counter-evidence
Tracking AI usage hours adds overhead, and the tracking itself can become performance theater (inflating logged hours to satisfy an executive rather than to inform a decision). In organizations without good data hygiene, logged hours may reflect what teams wish they were doing rather than actual usage patterns. The approach also assumes the executive will engage with data in good faith; where mandates are politically driven rather than outcome-driven, documentation may not change the conversation.
