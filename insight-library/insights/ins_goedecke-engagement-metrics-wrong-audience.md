# Optimizing for engagement metrics drives away the audience you actually want

## Claim
When writers optimize for engagement metrics (views, clicks, comments), they systematically attract audiences who reward viral posts and drive away readers who value careful thinking.

## Mechanism
Engagement metrics cannot distinguish a careful reader from a casual clicker. Both look identical in the data. Optimizing for the metric pushes content toward popular topics and provocative framings, which attracts people who enjoy viral content. As the writer delivers more of that, the careful readers drift away. The writer ends up serving an audience they never wanted: "audience capture," where the medium's incentive structure replaces the creator's actual goal. The remedy Goedecke names is "knowing what game you're playing." If the goal is a reputation among careful thinkers, views and engagement are proxy metrics that actively mislead.

## Conditions
Holds when: a writer publishes regularly in a medium with visible engagement metrics (social, newsletter open rates, YouTube views). The more frequently they check and respond to those metrics, the faster the drift occurs.

Fails when: the creator's actual goal IS viral reach, in which case engagement metrics are the right game to optimize for.

## Evidence
Goedecke published this observation on November 3, 2026, drawing on his own experience running a technical blog:

> "I'm replacing my audience of careful thinkers with a fake audience of clickers and sneerers."

He identifies the trap: "being captured by an audience you don't really even want." A writer who publishes a lament about industry changes draws clicks not because readers value the argument but because it gives them "an outlet for their worries." The click count goes up. The audience of careful thinkers goes down.

## Signals
- Comments skew toward reactions rather than engagement with the argument.
- Posts with broad appeal get more traffic than posts with tight, specific arguments.
- Writers find themselves gravitating to topics they know will travel, rather than topics they find genuinely interesting.

## Counter-evidence
The tradeoff is real but not universal. Ann Handley and other writing coaches argue that building a large audience first, then leading it toward careful content, is a viable path. Platforms with algorithmic amplification (LinkedIn, X/Twitter) favor engagement signals, so a writer who ignores them may never build a large enough readership to matter.
