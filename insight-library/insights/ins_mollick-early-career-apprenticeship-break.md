# AI breaks the traditional apprenticeship ladder from both ends, eliminating the feedback loop that built junior talent

## Claim
The traditional on-the-job apprenticeship model fails when both interns delegate to AI and managers prefer AI over interns for routine tasks. Neither side of the learning relationship functions as it used to, and the informal path from junior to senior practitioner collapses.

## Mechanism
The apprenticeship model depended on two simultaneous transfers. Interns did real work and built skill through deliberate practice under genuine stakes. Managers reviewed and corrected that work, building the feedback loop that transferred judgment. AI breaks both sides. Interns use AI to complete tasks faster, skipping the practice that builds understanding. Managers use AI because it is faster and more reliable than an intern for quick turnarounds. The result: interns carry credentials but not competence, and managers lose the habit of explaining their reasoning.

Mollick predicts that organizations will need formal training programs with explicit assessment to replace the informal path. This is a structural shift in how professional skills get transmitted across generations within a firm.

## Conditions
Holds when: junior employees have easy access to capable AI and face incentives to use it; managers face time pressure and AI that meets their quality bar; the skills being transmitted depend on accumulated practice, not just declarative knowledge.

Fails when: the work requires physical presence or tacit knowledge that AI cannot substitute; the organization has deliberately designed mentorship into the workflow with explicit assessment checkpoints; junior roles are defined by relationship and judgment work rather than task completion.

## Evidence
Mollick describes the dynamic in the Charter interview: interns use AI, managers turn to AI over interns, and the outcome is that no one is developing practical capability. He says companies that do not address this will find themselves in trouble over the next several years.

## Signals
- Interns completing work with AI output but unable to explain the decisions behind it
- Managers finding internal delegation to junior staff not worth the time investment
- Junior employees with strong credentials but limited tested judgment
- Senior staff who can no longer articulate the principles behind their decisions to others
- Companies launching formal certification programs for new hires as a replacement for on-the-job learning

## Counter-evidence
Some organizations report that AI compresses the junior-to-senior ramp by giving junior employees faster feedback, a reference point to interrogate, and better questions to ask senior staff. The loss depends on whether the tasks being skipped were genuinely formative or largely rote. Junior talent may develop differently rather than less well. Mollick's prediction is structural, not empirical: the pattern is early enough that the long-run effect is not yet visible in performance data.
