# Three-quarters of LinkedIn AI citations go to individual authors, not company pages

## Claim
Seventy-five percent of LinkedIn content cited by AI search engines is attributed to individual people, not company accounts, making employee and executive publishing a structurally stronger AI visibility channel than company-page posts on LinkedIn.

## Mechanism
LinkedIn's content URL structure encodes authorship at the account level. Individual-profile articles carry stronger topical-attribution signals than company-page posts for two reasons. First, AI citation engines weight individual author content as expert testimony, while company-page content reads as brand advertising. Second, LinkedIn hashtags used in individual articles propagate into URL path structure that AI search indexes for topical authority, independent of post engagement metrics. The result: a company-page post with high engagement earns less AI citation share than a personal article with lower reach, because the URL carries person-level expertise signals rather than brand-level promotional signals. Without explicit tagging of both the company and the author in the article, citations appear as "ghost citations," generating algorithm signal that never resolves back to the brand.

## Conditions
Holds when: the goal is AI search citation share on LinkedIn for B2B categories. Applies to any company where employees or executives can publish under personal accounts in a category with active AI-assisted search queries.

Fails when: the brand has no executives or employees willing to publish personally, when LinkedIn is not a relevant discovery channel for the category, or when the 75% figure reflects a sample not representative of the specific vertical (the data comes from Blatner's LinkedIn Ads-adjacent practice area).

## Evidence
Anthony Blatner (Speedwork Social), contributing to Exit Five Newsletter #275, August 13, 2026:

"75% of LinkedIn citations go to individual people, not company pages."

Testing methodology: Blatner launched freelinkedinadsaudit.com and ranked #1 in Google's AI search within one week by publishing the content as a LinkedIn Article tied to his personal profile. He documented a secondary tactic: repurpose 90% of existing blog posts as LinkedIn Articles (800-1,200 words), always tagging the company and people involved. Without explicit tagging, citations appear as what he calls "ghost citations," data for the algorithm that doesn't resolve back to the brand. He also recommends Google alerts for the brand and author name to track AI citation pickup within 24 hours of posting.

## Signals
- LinkedIn Articles published by individual employees earn more AI-search appearances than the same content published from the company page.
- Brand name appears in AI responses citing LinkedIn content when the article includes explicit company tagging; without it, the citation resolves to the author alone.
- Google alert monitoring shows AI search pickup of LinkedIn article content within 24 hours of posting.

## Counter-evidence
The 75% figure comes from Blatner's own testing, not an independent audit of LinkedIn citation distribution. His practice area (LinkedIn ads) may skew the data toward audiences where individual expertise is disproportionately valued. The strategy requires a pipeline of willing individual authors; companies with siloed communications policies or limited executive publishing programs cannot easily act on it. LinkedIn's algorithm and AI citation weighting may change, narrowing or eliminating the individual-versus-company gap. Blatner is presenting as a practitioner at Exit Five, not a researcher publishing with methodology.
