# When AI makes generation cheap, ranking and judgment about what matters become the scarce resource

## Claim
When everyone has access to the same AI models, raw generation capacity is no longer a differentiator. What remains scarce is the judgment to rank which outputs matter, rooted in a non-consensus perspective and accountability for a specific area.

## Mechanism
Generation tasks (writing, coding, summarizing) have clear outputs that AI can produce at volume. Once the tool is available to all, production capacity equalizes. The bottleneck shifts to selection: deciding which outputs are worth using, which questions are worth asking, and which signals matter in a specific context. Ranking requires a "non-consensus perspective," Shipper's term for a view shaped by deep context, responsibility for an outcome, and history with the problem. Those properties belong to the person embedded in the work, not to the model. The model can generate ten candidate insights from a customer survey; the person who owns the product and lives with the customer knows which one to act on.

## Conditions
Holds when: AI models are commoditized and widely accessible, equalizing production capacity across competitors. The task involves interpretation, prioritization, or decision-making rather than pure production. Multiple people working with the same AI draw different useful insights, confirming that the difference is in the person's perspective, not the model's output.
Fails when: a team has significantly worse AI access than competitors (capability gap, not judgment gap). The task requires production skill so rare that AI cannot match it, restoring production as the bottleneck.

## Evidence
Shipper draws the distinction through two measurement frames. The "aggregate view," built from benchmark trends, shows AI models improving faster than humans on standardized tests, projecting replacement. The "intimate view," built from how real people work, shows human judgment "riding on top of the same curve": as models improve, the people directing them also improve. The aggregate view freezes human skill at the 2020 baseline; the intimate view does not.

His concrete example: Every's team ran the same customer survey through Claude. Each person drew different insights from the output. The model could generate the findings; deciding which ones mattered required a person with accountability for a specific area.

> "Generation is really cheap, but ranking is hard."

## Signals
- Teams with identical AI access produce measurably different outcomes, with the gap traceable to the quality of direction rather than the volume of generation.
- Hiring criteria shift toward judgment, context depth, and accountability rather than production speed or tool familiarity.
- AI productivity gains compound faster for people with deep domain knowledge than for generalists with the same tools.

## Counter-evidence
The claim assumes judgment is not itself automatable in the near term. If models improve at open-ended ranking and prioritization, the gap narrows. Shipper's framing also applies most cleanly to knowledge work where the person is embedded in a specific domain over time; for tasks where generalist judgment is sufficient, the advantage of "non-consensus perspective" is smaller. The aggregate view Shipper dismisses does have predictive power for well-defined production tasks, even if it fails for judgment-heavy ones.
