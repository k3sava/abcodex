# AI credit pricing works like arcade money: costs feel less real until mass-market buyers stop tolerating it

## Claim
AI companies use invented currencies, undefined unit sizes, and large-number framing to make costs feel abstract, and this opacity works with early adopters but will block mass-market adoption until companies make costs "understandable, predictable, and aligned with customer value."

## Mechanism
Four forces compound the pricing opacity:
1. Invented currencies. Credits and tokens detach spending from real money the same way arcade tokens do. Customers spend credits without converting them back to dollars.
2. Large-number framing. "500 million tokens per week" feels generous because the baseline is never stated. The big number signals value without communicating it.
3. No settled unit of value. For general-purpose agents, the right unit (per message, per task, per hour, per outcome) remains unsettled across the industry. Companies avoid committing to a unit because it would expose cost uncertainty.
4. Unpredictable cost to serve. Agents with retries and multi-step plans vary widely in inference cost. Fraud and abused free tiers add further variance. Transparent pricing would expose this variance to competitive pressure.

Early adopters absorb ambiguity because novelty compensates. The mass market will not.

## Conditions
Holds when: the product is a general-purpose agent or AI assistant sold on a metered plan where customers cannot predict cost before committing.
Fails when: the product has a concrete, legible unit of value (audio minutes for voice generation, pages scraped for a web-scraping API). Those companies can price clearly and some already do.

## Evidence
Verna surveyed AI pricing pages in October 2026. Muse offered no logged-out pricing page; in-app, $20/month for 500 million tokens per week. Grok offered "4x Plus usage" for Premium and "15x" for Super, with no baseline stated.

> "Some companies promise 5x more usage without explaining 5x of WHAT."

> "We're now trying to price work that neither the customer nor the company can reliably predict."

ElevenLabs and Firecrawl are her counterexamples: ElevenLabs prices by audio minutes; Firecrawl by pages scraped and searches. Both are legible.

## Signals
- Your pricing page uses credits or tokens without a single concrete example of what one credit produces.
- Support volume includes frequent questions of the form "how much will X cost me?"
- Trial-to-paid conversion is strong among technical early adopters but drops in less technical segments.

## Counter-evidence
Credits are not purely an obfuscation tactic. They let companies bundle multiple models and tool types into a single unit and adjust for shifting inference costs without repricing plans. Verna acknowledges this: the problem is poor communication of what a credit buys, not the currency mechanism itself.
