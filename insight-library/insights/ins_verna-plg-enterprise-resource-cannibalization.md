# PLG companies that redirect resources from self-serve to enterprise sales destroy both channels because PLG creates the enterprise prospect pipeline

## Claim
When PLG companies deprioritize self-serve growth to pursue enterprise revenue, they cut the production function that generates their enterprise prospects. Enterprise teams then operate on a shrinking inbound base, both channels weaken simultaneously, and the move that was supposed to unlock revenue instead accelerates decline in both.

## Mechanism
In a PLG motion, individual users adopt the product independently, develop genuine expertise, and become internal champions who drive enterprise procurement. The enterprise channel depends on this pipeline. When a company shifts engineering, marketing, and customer success resources from self-serve to enterprise sales, it starves the activation loop that creates product-educated, internally-trusted champions. Enterprise teams then face prospects who are less familiar with the product and less enthusiastic about it, requiring more sales effort per deal while conversion rates fall.

The structural cause: individual-use adoption and enterprise procurement require incompatible go-to-market models, talent, and customer success philosophies. Forcing both from shared resources means each receives half the investment the model requires.

> "You can't ambush users and try to force/trick them into taking sales demos and call it product-led sales"

The misdiagnosis Verna names: PLG companies often see enterprise contracts as obviously higher-value and redirect resources accordingly, treating the $100K enterprise deal as more real than the 1,000 self-serve users generating the same revenue. But those 1,000 self-serve users are also the next quarter's enterprise pipeline. Cutting them cuts both.

## Conditions
Holds when: the PLG company has not built an alternative enterprise inbound source (outbound, partnerships, or brand awareness) sufficient to replace bottoms-up pipeline; the enterprise team is structured to capture inbound from PLG rather than generate its own outbound pipeline; and resource allocation between self-serve and enterprise is zero-sum.

Fails when: the company has strong enough outbound capability or brand recognition to generate enterprise pipeline independently of PLG; or the product was always enterprise-first and PLG was an experiment rather than the primary growth engine.

## Evidence
Verna describes this as a predictable, recurring failure pattern across PLG companies scaling toward enterprise. The Bill Murray "Groundhog Day" framing is explicit: she observes the same sequence repeatedly across different companies at different scales. She lists the pattern's symptoms: sales teams complaining about quota attainment, product teams getting redirected from self-serve features to enterprise packaging, and customer success being reoriented from activation to account management.

The mechanism is consistent enough that she describes the problems as "completely unoriginal" and says the solutions are also already known. The issue is not diagnosis but execution: companies recognize the trap after entering it, not before.

## Signals
- Sales team pushes for demos and gated features with users who adopted through self-serve
- Self-serve activation rate falls as product roadmap shifts toward enterprise feature packaging
- Enterprise pipeline thins 6 to 12 months after self-serve defunding because the new-user cohort that would have become champions never activated
- Customer success headcount shifts from onboarding and activation work to account management and renewal work

## Counter-evidence
PLG companies can layer enterprise sales on top of a strong PLG base without cannibalizing it when resource allocation is additive rather than zero-sum. Hiring enterprise-specific roles funded by new capital rather than redirecting existing PLG resources avoids the failure mode Verna describes. Companies with enough funding to staff both independently see different dynamics. The pattern she identifies specifically applies to companies treating resource allocation as zero-sum because of headcount constraints or funding pressure.
