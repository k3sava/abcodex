# Small AI-equipped teams at big firms are doing the work of dozens. No management playbook exists for the fights that follow.

## Claim
Scattered teams of 2-3 people at large enterprises are completing work at the volume previously requiring dozens of employees. The result is not celebration. It is conflict. Organizations have no established framework for handling a team that outperforms an entire department while crossing reporting lines.

## Mechanism
When individual cognitive output scales by a factor of ten or more, the headcount that existed to produce a given volume of work no longer reflects the actual work needed. Much of large-org headcount serves coordination, review, and approval functions rather than raw output capacity. A 2-3 person AI-equipped team can bypass those layers, delivering output directly. The organization's existing structure did not anticipate this configuration.

The fights that emerge are not about output quality. They are about ownership, territory, and implication. If a small team in one function delivers what another department usually owns, questions arise: who controls this output? What happens to the existing team? What does this mean for future headcount? Those questions have political answers, not technical ones. No playbook exists for them.

This pattern appears across sectors that differ in every other respect (manufacturing, financial services) but share the same organizational architecture: large headcount structured around coordination overhead rather than pure output capacity.

## Conditions
Holds when: the enterprise has existing teams whose scope overlaps with what the small AI team can produce, and when the small team crosses department or function boundaries rather than operating within a single silo.

Fails when: the AI-equipped team operates within a silo that owns its own output end-to-end, so there is no territorial conflict. Also fails when management has pre-established frameworks for cross-functional AI productivity.

## Evidence
Mollick reports this as a pattern heard from multiple large enterprises across sectors:

> "Thing I am hearing from a number of big firms, from manufacturers to financial services: we have scattered teams of 2-3 people using AI that are doing the work of dozens of folks in a fraction of the time, creating large-scale management fights over the implications and politic…"

The pattern spans at least two industries with different organizational cultures, limiting the chance this is an artifact of one firm's structure. The thread continues with the observation that no established management playbook exists for these configurations. The post drew 2.43K likes and 191 replies as of capture date, with responses confirming similar patterns from practitioners at other firms.

## Signals
- An AI-equipped small team delivers output that was previously produced by a larger team in a different function.
- HR, legal, or senior leadership is pulled into disputes with nothing to do with output quality.
- Teams request "rules of engagement" for AI-assisted cross-functional work before they will start.

## Counter-evidence
The pattern is anecdotal: Mollick is reporting from conversations, not systematic research. Selection bias is real: firms with these conflicts are more likely to surface them. Firms where small AI teams operate quietly within sanctioned scope would not appear as examples.

The "work of dozens" claim is unquantified. Without knowing the starting headcount, the AI task type, and the comparison baseline, the multiplier cannot be verified independently.
