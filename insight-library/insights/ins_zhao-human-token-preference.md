# Audiences prefer human-made work for the same reason they still watch human athletes, not AI simulations

## Claim
Even when AI can produce equivalent outputs, people may still prefer human-made work because they consume it as an expression of another person's perspective and experience.

## Mechanism
Sports and games are defined by human bodies and human judgment at play; an AI winning at chess destroys the category's value. The same logic may apply to written and creative work: what audiences actually consume is "human tokens": the perspective, decisions, and expression of another person. Not just the raw content. Zhao calls this becoming "consumers of other people's human tokens."

## Conditions
Holds when: The audience values authenticity, craft, or the idea that a specific human made this specific thing.
Fails when: The audience only cares about the outcome (a correct answer, a working piece of code) and not who or what produced it.

## Evidence
Backstage conversation at Every's Thesis: 2027 conference, reported by Grant Harvey.

> "We like to watch other meatball[s] play chess, not silicon play chess."

> "become consumers of other people's human tokens,"

> "We might like your expression, your poetry more than a language model's expression."

## Signals
- A product's growth splits when you separate "AI-generated for utility" from "human-made for expression" use cases; each serves a different demand.
- Creators who make their process visible (not just the output) retain audiences better when AI can produce similar outputs.

## Counter-evidence
The preference for human-made work may be a generational effect: audiences who grew up without AI-generated content prefer human artifacts, but it is not clear the pattern holds for generations that grew up with both options available. Preference for human chess players did not persist after AlphaGo made AI chess dominance visible and undeniable.
