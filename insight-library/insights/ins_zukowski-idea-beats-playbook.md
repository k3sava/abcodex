# A game's core idea is the primary driver of marketing outcomes; identical playbooks diverge based on idea quality

## Claim
Running the same marketing playbook on two different games produces dramatically different outcomes depending on the strength of the underlying game idea. Marketing amplifies appeal; it cannot manufacture it.

## Mechanism
Steam's algorithmic widgets deliver impressions consistently to the games they feature. But impression-to-wishlist conversion and wishlist-to-purchase conversion are controlled by the game idea, not by how the game is positioned on a store page. When the Steam Personal Calendar widget shows "Bills Must Be Paid," users who see it either wishlist immediately or scroll past. The widget cannot change that decision. Marketing execution determines who sees the game; the game idea determines what they do when they see it. Zukowski observed the same widget delivering 1,720 wishlists per day for a game with a compelling hook and far fewer for games with weaker concepts under identical positioning conditions.

## Conditions
Holds when: the marketing execution is competent (correct tags, clear screenshots, genre-accurate description). Marketing quality is not at issue.
Fails when: a fundamentally poor marketing execution (wrong tags, misleading screenshots, wrong price tier) is suppressing an otherwise compelling idea. In that case, execution is the variable.

## Evidence
Zukowski analyzed July 2026 Steam bestseller data and observed that the games that performed best were distinguished first by their game concept, not their marketing spend or sophistication. He concluded:

> "The biggest lesson is that the idea matters more than anything else. You can polish, market and optimize a game forever, but if the core idea isn't interesting enough, it's very hard to compensate for that."

He also noted that the genre itself evolves: "players will expect more quality and more uniqueness, because the baseline keeps rising." The bar for what constitutes a compelling idea rises with the market.

## Signals
- Identical campaigns for two games in the same genre produce conversion rates an order of magnitude apart.
- Post-mortem analyses of underperforming games consistently cite game concept or execution problems, not marketing execution.
- Viral moments for good games follow discovery through any channel; viral moments for weak games do not convert.

## Counter-evidence
Early-stage games without any audience struggle to test whether their idea is compelling because they lack distribution. Marketing can create enough visibility to run the test, but the hypothesis still depends on the idea.
