# Deploying AI tools company-wide is not the same as changing how the company works

## Claim
Distributing AI tools across a workforce is a procurement and training exercise. Restructuring how work is organized, which decisions get made where, and how workflows cross departmental and system boundaries is a separate, slower process that follows a different historical pattern.

## Mechanism
Every major platform shift began with a tool on every desk. Spreadsheets, browsers, and now Copilot or ChatGPT spread widely before the deeper organizational change followed. Real transformation came later, through systems that rebuilt how work was organized: SAP and Oracle in finance, ERP across operations. The trap in AI adoption is the same one companies fell into with earlier platforms. Company-wide licences give every employee access to AI but cannot change the structure of the work they do. Pilots can restructure one process, but they do not scale on their own. The gap between diffusion and transformation closes only when leadership separates three distinct questions:

1. How do we buy, build, and deploy this? (Procurement and culture.)
2. How does it change our operations?
3. Does it change our product and our market?

Failing to separate these questions lets organizations measure progress by licence count or user activation and mistake that for structural change. Evans names writing code as a leading example: writing code was never the hard part. Knowing what to build, and changing workflows that cross departments, systems, and regulations, is.

## Conditions
Holds when: an organization defines success by user adoption metrics alone without tracking workflow restructuring or decision-making changes.
Fails when: an organization is small enough that tool adoption and process change happen simultaneously, or when the domain is narrow enough that a tool directly substitutes for the entire prior workflow without structural design.

## Evidence
Public cloud adoption is Evans' benchmark for the pace of organizational change. Goldman Sachs CIO survey data shows public cloud still runs only about 30% of enterprise workloads after two decades of availability. Evans frames organizational change as an industry, not a project.

Ramp spending data from the November 2026 presentation: the top 1% of firms spend over $10,000 per employee per month on AI. The median firm spends about $16. About 45% of US workers use generative AI at work, but fewer than 15% use it daily.

Deloitte UK data: about two-thirds of AI users have employer permission, only about a third have employer-paid tools, and about half have had any training. Evans calls this the "spray-and-pray" phase of deployment.

## Signals
- Licence count rising while measured workflow decisions stay unchanged.
- Copilot or ChatGPT adoption reported as a success metric without any operational metric attached.
- Pilot programs generating case studies that never scale to other teams.
- The same meetings, the same approval chains, and the same handoff structures as before AI adoption.

## Counter-evidence
Some organizations have used company-wide tool roll-outs as the entry point for genuine structural redesign. A tool spreading widely can surface the processes worth redesigning by showing which tasks are easiest to delegate. The historical comparison to cloud is imperfect: AI changes work directly, while cloud changed where it ran. The pace could differ.
