# Operator-creators who declare conflicts upfront and put the day job first sustain dual roles without collisions

## Claim
A full-time operator who builds a public content brand faces four distinct conflict types: IP ownership, competitive optics, sponsored content muddying the professional channel, and audience conflation of personal opinion with employer position. Each requires a specific rule, not a general disclosure. The day job comes first in every priority conflict; content slips, the job does not.

## Mechanism
Verna names five operating rules for the dual role:

One: Disclose before signing, not after. Review the employment agreement for IP ownership, confidentiality scope, and conflict-of-interest clauses before publishing or entering sponsorships. Carve out pre-existing content and side businesses explicitly at the offer stage, not reactively.

Two: Separate channels by incentive. Sponsored posts on a professional platform blur the signal readers use to weight the operator's advice. Verna routes sponsorships through the newsletter only, keeping the main professional channel free of visible commercial incentives.

Three: Set a visible priority. When travel, deadlines, or a product launch collide with a content commitment, the day job wins. Stating this publicly sets the audience's expectations rather than leaving them to infer it from inconsistent publishing cadence.

Four: Map conflict types beyond direct competitors. Partners, vendors, customers, and acquisition targets all create potential conflicts. The category is wider than most operators assume.

Five: Apply the bail-out heuristic. When a deal is technically permissible but feels ambiguous, decline it. The cost of a declined sponsorship is recoverable. The cost of a perception conflict is not.

> "I'm like a one-woman distribution channel."

> "when in doubt, bail out"

The asymmetry that makes the dual role worth structuring: day-job experience makes content more credible, while audience-building creates distribution that employers value for brand, product awareness, and recruiting. Both sides gain, but only if the operator never needs to choose between them in public.

## Conditions
Holds when: the operator holds a senior, visible role (head of growth, head of PMM, VP) where their professional opinion carries market weight; the employer supports employee content creation explicitly; the content domain overlaps with but does not directly compete with the employer's products.
Fails when: the employment agreement restricts public commentary on the industry; the employer treats any external content as a distraction or potential liability; the operator's content is in a domain with no relationship to their day job (separation is total, so the amplification benefit disappears).

## Evidence
Verna draws entirely on her own experience across multiple operator roles, most recently as Head of Growth at Lovable. Lovable's "build in public" culture is cited as an example of an employer who actively supports employee content. No external data is provided. The strength of the claim rests on Verna's track record as a credible growth operator whose public writing is treated as authoritative in the PLG community.

## Signals
- You have accepted a sponsorship without checking whether the sponsor is a vendor, customer, or acquisition target of your employer
- Your audience has quoted your personal opinion in the press as your company's position
- A professional post with sponsor attribution has received significantly lower engagement than your unsponsored posts
- You have a content calendar commitment you know you will miss because of a work deadline, and you have not told your audience

## Counter-evidence
The playbook assumes the operator has built a large enough audience that employers see it as a recruiting and brand asset. Early-stage creators with small audiences may not receive the same employer latitude, and the disclosure conversation carries more risk before the audience exists. The rules also assume a US-style employment context; non-compete and IP laws vary significantly across jurisdictions and may make the "carve out at offer stage" advice impractical.
